# Do synthetic callers change which model wins? A small measurement

> **Update (13/10/2026).** After this note was first shared I ran follow-ups on the same data (FLEURS as a second human set, a check of TTS rendering errors, calibration with a few human recordings, a rerun of the noise-only test with Whisper's standard settings, and published statistical checks). Some claims below did not hold as written. I kept the original text and marked each change with "(corrected)", with the old wording struck through. What changed and why:
> 1. **Averaging voices (item 1).** I wrote that averaging "manufactured certainty" without removing "the bias". A bias is neither shown nor excluded: the lab-minus-human difference is not significant on any condition. What is shown is over-precision: the interval held the 7 voices fixed and is about half as wide as it is when voices are resampled too. With that fix and a multiple-testing correction, the average still favours Parakeet on 2 of the 3 noisy conditions, in the same direction as the human estimate, which 250 human sentences can neither confirm nor rule out.
> 2. **Whisper on pure noise (item 4).** "33 of 100" came from a raw decoder with non-standard input padding. With Whisper's standard `transcribe` settings (mlx-whisper 0.4.3), it wrote text on 95 to 100 % of the same kind of noise clips. Its no-speech gate never fired. A default Silero VAD cut this to 2 to 3 %. The problem is larger than I reported.
> 3. **"Not uniformly too easy" (item 3) depends on the corpus.** Against FLEURS readers, every voice except MMS was easier than humans, and every voice understated the cost of noise. The MMS rendering-error share is "a third to about two thirds" depending on the rule, from a flag nobody has checked by listening.
> 4. **"Gaps against humans are upper bounds" (limits)** is withdrawn: against a harder human set the lab looked more optimistic, not less.
> 5. **Scope added** to item 2 (descriptive range, in-domain control), to the PPI point (about 20 distinct speakers, WER level only) and to the limits (non-standard Whisper padding in the main run).
> 6. **Prior work.** The design itself is not new; see the last line under "Limits".
>
> Full reconciliation: `CLAIMS-STATUS.md`. Follow-ups in plain words: `FOLLOWUPS.md`.

I got curious about the sim-to-real gap in voice-agent testing: simulated callers do not sound or behave like real ones, so a change can look better in the lab than in production. I measured one slice of it, the acoustic slice, with public models on a laptop. Here is what came out. It is one setup, read speech and simulated phone lines, so it may or may not transfer to your stack.

**Setup in one paragraph.** 250 LibriSpeech sentences read by real people and by 7 TTS voices (Kokoro x2, Piper x2, Meta MMS, macOS x2; 4 engines), plus 122 written caller scripts read by the same TTS voices. Every clip goes through 10 simulated channels (G.711 phone line, Opus, G.722, street and crowd noise at 10 and 5 dB, packet loss, muffled audio, and a "realistic" mix). Three public ASR models stand in for the agent's ear: Whisper large-v3-turbo, Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2, and wav2vec2-base as a weak, in-domain control. About 74,000 scored transcriptions.

## The numbers that would matter for a decision

1. **No single voice confidently flipped the winner between two close models, ~~but averaging voices manufactured certainty~~ but averaging voices gave intervals that were too narrow (corrected).** On real speech, Parakeet beats Whisper only on clean audio (by 0.37 WER points [0.12, 0.67]); on the phone and noisy channels the real difference is within noise, meaning the human sample cannot tell, not that there is no difference (corrected). No voice's lab-vs-reality difference survived correction across 28 decisions. The average of all 7 voices, though, declared Parakeet significantly better on all 3 phone and noisy conditions; with voices resampled and a correction for the 4 conditions, on 2 of 3 (corrected). ~~Averaging many renderings of the same sentence narrows the interval without removing the bias.~~ Averaging many renderings of the same sentence narrows the interval; a bias is neither shown nor excluded (corrected).

2. **The voice changes how big a difference looks.** For a pair with a large real gap (Whisper vs wav2vec2 on the realistic channel, 21.7 points on human speech), the lab said 9.6 points with Kokoro and 35.8 with macOS Daniel. The winner never flipped, but the size did, by 0.37x to 3.72x across cells (corrected: a descriptive range; every such pair involves the in-domain wav2vec2 control; on FLEURS the lab always understated, 0.26x to 0.92x).

3. **Synthetic speech is not uniformly "too easy" (corrected: on LibriSpeech; on FLEURS every voice except MMS was easier than humans).** On clean audio Kokoro was slightly easier than humans (-0.2 to -0.4 points), Piper ryan harder (+0.8 to +1.4) and Meta MMS clearly harder (+3.6 to +4.6, ~~about half of it~~ a third to about two thirds of it, depending on the cleaning rule, word-level rendering errors flagged from transcripts, not checked by listening (corrected)). Where it misleads most is noise: Kokoro showed about half the WER increase that the same noise caused on human speech (Whisper, phone + 10 dB noise: real +2.1 points, Kokoro understated it by 1.2 [0.3, 2.1]).

4. **Emergency keywords under a bad phone line.** On the realistic channel, Whisper caught 95.5 % of keyword instances ("bleeding", "not breathing", "chest pain", "help", ...) and Parakeet 94.6 %; at call level they missed 7 and 9 of 546 renderings. The weak model caught 55 %. Two small surprises: macOS Daniel says "hell" for "help" often enough to make "help" the worst keyword, and Whisper produced text ("thank you", mostly) on ~~33 of 100 clips of pure noise~~ 95 to 100 % of clips of pure noise after the phone line with its standard settings; 33 of 100 was a raw decoder with non-standard padding (corrected).

## Two statistics points I found useful

- **Zero misses is not zero risk.** With 105 keyword instances and no miss, the rule of three only bounds the miss rate below about 3 %, and that assumes independent instances, which keywords in the same script are not.
- **A few real recordings go a long way (PPI).** Recording about 25 scripts with real people and running every script through TTS gave a valid interval on real-speech WER, 9 to 23 % narrower than the 25 recordings alone. Human-only intervals at 10 recordings under-covered (80 to 89 %). The gain is modest because per-sentence agreement between synthetic and human results was only 0.5 to 0.7, and it needs scripted calls with a synthetic twin. (Corrected: this was validated with about 19 distinct speakers per 25 recordings; one person reading all 25 gives that person's WER. The gain is for the WER level only, not for a model-vs-model difference.)

## Limits, plainly

- Behaviour is not measured: every voice reads the same script. No panic, hesitation, off-script answers or barge-in. That half of the gap is probably the larger one.
- "Human" is audiobook read speech, which is close to the training data of these models. ~~Gaps against humans are upper bounds;~~ Gaps against LibriSpeech readers are neither upper nor lower bounds: against FLEURS readers the voices looked easier and the lab more optimistic (corrected); the clean fix is real phone recordings of the caller scripts.
- Offline ASR on single utterances, simulated codecs and noise, Whisper on 4 of 10 channels, and nobody listened to the clips. Main-run Whisper WERs used non-standard window padding; the effect on speech WER is not measured (corrected).
- Prior work (added): seven voices against a matched human reading is not new (Lau et al., ISSTA 2023; 2M-BELEBELE on FLEURS); Whisper's "thank you" on non-speech and Silero as a guard are in Barański et al., ICASSP 2025; the averaging issue is Clark (1973) and Deutsch, Dror and Roth (TACL 2021). Full list: `RELATED-WORK.md`.

Code, tables and all caveats: `README.md` and `results/FINDINGS.md`. Happy to be told where this does not match what you see with real traffic.

## Corrections

**13/10/2026, on item 1.** The interval behind "averaging voices manufactured certainty" resampled speakers but held the 7 voices fixed. Resampling the voices too (BOOT-BOTH, the standard fix when the test items are themselves a sample; Deutsch, Dror and Roth, TACL 2021) makes it about twice as wide. The average still favours Parakeet on the noisy channels, in the same direction as the human estimate, and the lab-minus-real difference is not significant. The 250 human sentences are too few to confirm or rule out a gap of this size: about 1 point is detectable at 80 % power, and the lab says 0.75 to 0.86. So "manufactured certainty" overstates what the data show. The correct reading: the averaged lab claims a winner that the human sample cannot check, and its v2 interval was too narrow. No voice, and no average, ever picked the wrong winner with confidence. Details: `results/followups/literature_checks/NOTES.md`.

**13/10/2026, later the same day: completing the first entry, and the other items.**
- *Item 1.* The lab-minus-human offsets are +0.28 [-0.13, +0.69], +0.38 [-0.40, +1.05] and +0.15 [-0.48, +0.74] points on phone, phone+noise10 and realistic. All point estimates lean toward Parakeet and the upper bounds reach about 1 point, so a bias is neither shown nor excluded. The 7-voice average was not corrected for multiple tests in the original run; with voices resampled and Holm over the 4 conditions it stays significant on phone+noise10 and realistic, not on phone. On FLEURS (realistic channel) the human sample shows Parakeet ahead, +1.23 [0.36, 2.10], a result that is fragile to reader clustering; the averaged lab agrees in sign at about a quarter of the size, and its interval includes 0 once voices are resampled. Every Whisper number here, human side included, used the main run's decoder (see item 4); the comparisons are internally consistent but may differ from off-the-shelf Whisper. Sources: `results/followups/calibration`, `results/followups/fleurs`, `results/followups/literature_checks`.
- *Item 4.* The 33 of 100 used Whisper's raw decoder: greedy, no temperature fallback, no no-speech gate, and a 30 s window padded with the log-mel of digital silence rather than the way `transcribe()` pads it. With `mlx_whisper.transcribe` 0.4.3 defaults on the same 100 clips: 95 [88.7, 98.4]; at random lengths of 3 to 6 s: 100 [96.4, 100]. 80 of the 95 were "thank you". Changing only the padding gives 94. The no-speech gate fired on 0 clips (no_speech_prob about 1e-10), shown for mlx-whisper 0.4.3 only; openai-whisper and faster-whisper were not tested. A default Silero VAD cut the rate to 2 and 3 of 100 and dropped none of 1,000 human clips. Scope: 200 clips, four noise types at one level. On a larger non-speech set, Parakeet produced text on about 30 % of short (0.5 to 2 s) clips and about 2 % of long ones, excluding the bot's own echoed prompt. No model invented an emergency keyword outside that echo. Source: `results/followups/phantom/v2/NOTES.md`.
- *Item 3.* On FLEURS, every non-MMS voice was easier than humans in 60 of 60 cells (59 of 60 if the intervals are widened 2x for reader clustering), and every voice understated noise damage in 43 of 49 cells (36 at 2x, 35 in the cleanest third of recordings). FLEURS recordings carry their own noise and some readers deviate from the reference text, which inflates human WER. For MMS, symmetric cleaning of flagged words removes 35 to 45 % of its gap, a stricter rule 49 to 63 %; the flag was checked on paper only (30 items, 77 % [58, 90] plausible, no audio). Sources: `results/followups/fleurs`, `results/followups/tts_errors`.
- *Item 2, PPI and limits:* as marked inline above. The 7 voices come from 4 engines and are not independent draws.
