# Related work

This section places the repo in the published literature. Each theme says what is already known, then how this repo relates to it: whether it replicates, extends or differs. Numbers in brackets point to the reference list at the end. I checked every reference on its arXiv, proceedings, publisher or catalog page on 13/10/2026. Where two readings of a paper disagreed, I kept only the figures that both supported. A few points come only from abstracts; the text says so. A second pass on the same day, written as a completeness review from the speech side, added references 106 to 149 (each checked on arXiv, Crossref, the publisher page or the model card) and rewrote the novelty section. Its web search budget ran out early, so its 2025-2026 coverage rests on papers checked by identifier; the searches still worth running are listed at the end of "What we should have cited or adopted".

Where this repo stands, in one paragraph: almost every ingredient is already published. TTS test sets can reorder ASR models. TTS audio is usually easier than human speech. TTS-induced ASR failures are often artefacts of the voice. Cross-ASR agreement can single out TTS faults. Whisper invents "thank you" on non-speech. Telephone codecs, packet loss and noise at a set SNR are standard perturbations. PPI and active inference already give valid intervals from a few labels. Several TTS voices read against a matched human reading of the same LibriSpeech sentences is not new either: Lau et al. [58] did it with four TTS engines and five ASRs, and zero-shot TTS papers report their WER next to a ground-truth human row [127, 128]. The danger of averaging over a sample of stimuli is a fifty-year-old result in psycholinguistics [106]. What does not seem to be published is the combination: the voice treated as a factor that changes *comparisons between ASR models*, measured against a matched human reading of the same sentences, crossed with telephone channels, with cluster bootstrap, a difference-in-differences test and multiplicity control, and with the question "does the lab pick the same winner as reality?" asked directly.

## 1. Synthetic speech as ASR test data

**What is known.** Several papers already ran the same text through humans and through TTS and found that TTS flatters the models. In the speech-aware dialogue track of DSTC11 [2], a baseline ASR scored 8.2 % WER on TTS against 13.0 % on human readings of the same user turns, and Whisper scored 4.6 % against 8.9 %. Human audio had many more deletions. The authors concluded that TTS cannot stand in for human speech. In Kubis et al. [4], Whisper scored 11.2 to 11.7 % on TTS against 16.3 % on real recordings. In WildASR [5], Whisper large-v3 scored 3.7 % on TTS against 21.7 % on real child speech with identical transcripts. VoiceBench [3] found that every voice assistant scored higher on synthetic than on real recordings of the same instructions. Rankings still agreed fairly well (Kendall tau 0.87 between real and synthetic, Kendall's W 0.83 across five TTS renderings), but item-level agreement was weak (Spearman 0.39 to 0.71), and one system gained about 50 % on synthetic audio. VoxEval [129] renders MMLU questions with all six OpenAI TTS voices and finds that scores vary little across voices; WildSpeech-Bench [130] uses 16 CosyVoice voice profiles mixed with ESC-50 noise. Neither compares its TTS audio with human recordings of the same text.

The closest precedent for our ranking question is 2M-BELEBELE [1]. It rendered FLEURS with MMS-TTS, ran three ASR systems on human and synthetic FLEURS, and counted rank swaps. In French and Spanish the top two systems swapped places, and Whisper lost more on MMS-TTS audio than its competitor did. The authors concluded that synthetic speech benchmarks may not be reliable. Their result rests on one TTS per language and single WER point estimates, with gaps of 0.5 to 1 point and no CI or test.

The multi-voice, human-anchored design itself has precedents. Lau et al. [58] rendered LibriSpeech and LJ Speech text with four TTS engines (Google TTS, Espeak, Festival, GlowTTS), transcribed it with five ASRs including wav2vec2, and compared every TTS x ASR cell with the human recording of the same text. Average WER was 0.099 on human LibriSpeech audio against 0.282 on TTS, the gap depended strongly on the engine, and one ASR (Vosk) did better on TTS than on human LibriSpeech audio, an ordering inversion they set aside as an anomaly. They report no CI or test on differences between ASRs. Zero-shot TTS papers use the same design with the roles reversed, the ASR being a fixed judge: F5-TTS [128], following the Seed-TTS evaluation protocol [127], transcribes LibriSpeech-PC test-clean with Whisper-large-v3 and reports 2.23 % WER for the human recordings against 2.42 to 3.59 % for TTS systems. Many TTS voices against a human reading of the same sentences is therefore routine. What these papers do not ask is whether the voice changes the comparison between two ASR models.

Speech Robust Bench [6] treats TTS speech as one of 114 perturbations of LibriSpeech. Its synthetic-speech degradation is small for Parakeet and Whisper and large for wav2vec2, which matches the ordering we see. Kubis et al. [4] add a useful counterpoint: for coarse NLU robustness scores, TTS was an adequate proxy (mean difference 0.03), even though its WER was optimistic.

On calibration, Wang et al. [7] already anchor synthetic WER on matched real WER on FLEURS ("Normalized Intelligibility"). Minixhofer et al. [8, 9, 10] measure the distribution distance between TTS and real speech (TTSDS, TTSDS2), and TTSDS2 tracks human ratings better than 15 other metrics. Rossenbach et al. [11] warn that MOS-style naturalness scores did not predict how useful a TTS was for ASR (in training). Yang et al. [12] show that some commercial TTS voices are more intelligible in noise than human voices; the older literature on rule-based synthesis found synthetic speech less intelligible to human listeners than natural speech [137], so the direction depends on the generation of TTS, not on TTS as such. Taylor and Richmond [13] established that ASR-based evaluation of TTS needs bootstrap CIs and a few hundred stimuli before significance settles.

More broadly, which ASR model looks best depends on the test set. Likhomanenko et al. [124] argue that one benchmark is not enough and that WER averaged over many benchmarks is a good proxy for real-world noisy data; ESB [125] and the Open ASR Leaderboard [126] (86 systems, 12 datasets) made multi-dataset reporting the norm. A synthetic voice is one more test-set choice, and our results are one case of this wider problem.

**How this repo relates.**
- *Replicates* the optimism of TTS for some voices: Kokoro is easier than human speech in 31 of 48 cells.
- *Qualifies* it: five of our seven voices are harder than human speech, so "TTS is easier" is a property of the voice, not of TTS.
- *Extends* 2M-BELEBELE [1] from one TTS to seven voices from four engines (two Kokoro voices from one StyleTTS 2 model [145]; two Piper voices and MMS, all VITS models [146, 147]; two macOS voices), and Lau et al. [58] from per-cell WER to inference on model gaps, with a speaker-cluster bootstrap, a DiD test and Holm correction. With that inference, no single-voice flip between the two close models survives correction. A one-voice, no-CI flip like theirs is therefore weak evidence. Our MMS result points the same way as theirs (MMS distorts Whisper vs Parakeet the most), and our FLEURS run uses their corpus.
- *Extends* VoiceBench [3]. Its human anchor (real vs synthetic CommonEval) uses one TTS, and its five-voice comparison, like VoxEval's six voices [129], has no human anchor. We measure how much each voice rescales an apparent gap relative to human speech (0.37x to 3.72x, on pairs that involve the in-domain wav2vec2 control), and show that averaging voices with the voices held fixed gives a winner, with a too-narrow interval, that the human sample can neither confirm nor rule out (updated 13/10/2026 after BOOT-BOTH and FLEURS; an earlier wording said "a confident winner where human speech has none"; see `CLAIMS-STATUS.md`, R1d). VoiceBench reads high agreement across TTS sets as reliability; our averaging result says agreement between voices is not agreement with humans.
- *Differs* from Speech Robust Bench [6]: there, TTS is a corruption to be robust to; here, it is a stand-in whose fidelity is measured.
- *Shares the convention* of TTS evaluation [127, 128], a ground-truth row next to TTS rows, with the roles swapped: there the ASR is a fixed judge of the TTS; here the TTS is the instrument, and the question is whether it preserves comparisons between ASR models.
- *New as far as I found:* crossing channels with TTS vs human. No paper measures whether a channel's cost estimated on TTS matches its cost on human speech for the same sentences. VoiceBench [3], VoxEval [129] and WildSpeech-Bench [130] add noise or channel effects to TTS audio, but never to a matched human reading. Our result that clean Kokoro shows about half the noise damage fits Yang et al. [12] but has no direct precedent.

## 2. Simulated callers and simulator fidelity

**What is known.** Voice-agent benchmarks now drive agents with synthetic callers. tau-Voice [14] voices an LLM user with a commercial TTS and applies a "realistic" channel: G.711 mu-law at 8 kHz, noise at 15 dB SNR, burst noise, muffling, and Gilbert-Elliott frame drops. That channel is almost the same as ours. EVA-Bench [15] uses one accent voice and coffee-shop noise, and admits that its accent effect may partly reflect that particular voice. VAmoS Bench [16] has no channel simulation and lists codecs and packet loss as future work. Full-Duplex-Bench [17] found that a system interrupts more often on real audio than on TTS audio (takeover rate 0.310 vs 0.255). None of these validates its caller audio against human recordings, and all say so in their limitations.

On the behavioural side, paired human-vs-simulator studies exist. In Mind the Sim2Real Gap [18], 451 people ran tau-bench: agents succeeded 63.6 % of the time with humans and up to 77.8 % with LLM simulators. Lost in Simulation [19] found up to 9 points of swing depending on the simulator LLM, with miscalibration that depends on task difficulty, and proposed an ECE-style calibration metric. SimulatorArena [20] reports simulator-to-human rank agreement around Spearman 0.7. Li et al. [21] found that static audio benchmarks correlate poorly with interactive human preferences (rho at most 0.49).

The older literature gives metrics for this problem. Sim2Real predictivity [22] defines a sim-vs-real correlation coefficient (SRCC) and shows that tuning the simulator raised it from 0.18 to 0.844. Williams [23] proposes a Cramer-von Mises divergence between real and simulated score distributions. The classic statistical user simulators [24] fitted their ASR-error channel to real recognizer output, a step that modern TTS-plus-channel benchmarks dropped. CHiME-3 [25] asked our exact question for noise and microphones: does simulated test data preserve how methods behave on real data? The REVERB challenge [121] scored every system on simulated (SimData) and real (RealData) reverberant recordings side by side, and the 2020 DNS Challenge [122] reported that models that do well on a synthetic test set often "degrade significantly on real recordings". In robotics, SIMPLER [120] pairs simulated and real evaluations of the same policies and proposes the Mean Maximum Rank Violation (MMRV), a rank-violation score weighted by the real performance margin, because Pearson correlation can miss ranking errors.

**How this repo relates.**
- *Complements* tau-Voice, EVA-Bench and VAmoS. We measure the confound they admit: voice identity, and the absence of a human-audio check. tau-Voice's per-provider accent effects come from one TTS persona per accent, exactly the kind of comparison our 0.37x to 3.72x voice scale factor puts in doubt.
- *Is the acoustic twin* of Mind the Sim2Real Gap [18] and Lost in Simulation [19]. They study the behavioural half with real humans; we study the acoustic half with read speech.
- *Inherits* the CHiME-3 framing [25]. "Does the lab pick the same winner?" is not a new question; asking it about synthetic voices on telephone channels is.
- *Should adopt* SRCC [22], MMRV [120], the ECE-style metric [19] and the Cramer-von Mises divergence [23] rather than inventing fidelity metrics.

## 3. Statistics for comparing a proxy with reality

**What is known.** Machine translation meta-evaluation reports pairwise accuracy: how often a metric orders a system pair the way humans do [26]. Mathur et al. [27] show that metric-human correlations are fragile with few or outlying systems, and propose counting Type I and Type II decision errors on thresholded differences. Deutsch et al. [28] recommend resampling both systems and inputs (BOOT-BOTH) for CIs on metric correlations, and the matching permutation test (PERM-BOTH). Perlitz et al. [29] show that agreement statistics computed over too few models lead to invalid conclusions. Miller [30], Bisani and Ney [31] and Liu and Peng [32] cover paired comparisons, bootstrap CIs for WER and speaker clustering. Chatzi et al. [33] show that rankings built only from LLM judges are often inconsistent with human preferences, and output prediction-powered rank-sets instead.

An older literature, outside NLP, bears directly on averaging voices. Clark [106] named the "language-as-fixed-effect fallacy": treating a sample of stimuli as fixed when the conclusion is meant to generalize to other stimuli, which makes tests anti-conservative. Crossed random effects for subjects and items [107, 108], the same argument in social psychology [109], power formulas for designs in which samples of participants respond to samples of stimuli [110], generalizability theory [112] and the "generalizability crisis" [111] follow from it. LLM evaluation met the same problem with prompt templates: single-template benchmarks give brittle results, and multi-prompt evaluation treats templates as a sampled factor [113]. In ASR, the classic paired tests are Gillick and Cox's [114], and Liu, Veliche and Peng [115] model WER differences between speaker groups with mixed-effects Poisson regression to absorb speaker heterogeneity. Information retrieval measures ranking stability as a swap rate: how often two topic sets order a system pair differently [116]. Card et al. [117] show that many NLP comparisons are underpowered, Dror et al. [119] review significance testing in NLP, and equivalence tests (TOST) [118] are the standard way to support "no difference" instead of reading it off a CI that includes 0.

**How this repo relates.**
- *Already does* paired, speaker-clustered bootstrap CIs, which matches [30, 31, 32].
- *With 3 ASR models,* Kendall tau takes only four values. A rank correlation over models is uninformative [29], so the pairwise DiD with Holm stays the primary test. That design choice is supported by the literature, not a weakness of ours.
- *Should add* pairwise decision accuracy [26] and a Type I / Type II table [27]. Our "averaging manufactures certainty" result is a Type I error in Mathur's sense: the lab declares a winner where humans see none.
- *Should treat voices as a sampled factor* when averaging (BOOT-BOTH [28]). Our 7-voice mean was bootstrapped over speakers only, which made its CI too narrow. Update (13/10/2026, `results/followups/literature_checks`): BOOT-BOTH widens it 1.6x to 2.3x, but the winner remains on 2 or 3 of 3 noisy conditions; the rest of the gap is low power on the human side.
- *Parallels* Chatzi et al. [33]: proxy-only rankings look confident but disagree with humans. Ours is the acoustic version, for TTS voice panels.
- *Is an instance of the language-as-fixed-effect fallacy* [106]. Averaging seven voices and resampling only speakers treats the voices as fixed, so the CI leaves out voice-to-voice variance. The published fixes are BOOT-BOTH [28] or a mixed model with crossed random effects [107, 108, 115]. The voices are also nested in engines (one Kokoro model, three VITS models, one macOS engine), so the effective number of independent voices is nearer four than seven.
- *Infers "no winner" from a CI that includes 0.* "Real speech shows no winner" (for example +0.60 [-0.04, +1.29] points on realistic, in the same direction as the 7-voice mean) is absence of evidence. It needs an equivalence test [118] with a stated smallest effect of interest, or the wording "the human data cannot confirm a winner". The same holds for "no single voice flips": report the minimum detectable DiD [117].
- *Should name* our decision flips by their IR name, the swap rate [116].

## 4. Prediction-powered inference with a few human recordings

**What is known.** PPI [34] combines a small labelled set with predictions on a large unlabelled set and gives valid intervals whatever the predictor. PPI++ [35] adds a power-tuning weight lambda. Cross-prediction-powered inference [36] learns the predictor on the labelled data by cross-fitting without breaking validity. AutoEval [37] applies PPI and PPI++ to model evaluation with synthetic labels and raises the effective human sample size by up to 50 %. Eyre and Madras [38] show that PPI++ is OLS in disguise and can be worse than classical inference when labels are scarce; ridge-shrunk variants fix this. Dorner et al. [39] bound the gain: the sample-efficiency factor is at most 1/(1 - rho^2). For binary outcomes where the judge is no more accurate than the evaluated model, that caps the gain at 2x. Chaganty et al. [40] found only a 7 to 13 % cost reduction from debiasing automatic metrics with control variates.

Mozer [41] shows that the PPI mean estimator is the survey-sampling difference estimator, and that PPI++ is the GREG estimator. StratPPI [42] and Mozer, Pashley and Miratrix [43] stratify by the surrogate and allocate labels Neyman-style. MultiPPI [44] and PPI by mixture of experts [45] combine several proxies; the mixture paper picks the weights that minimize the variance of the rectified estimator. Bayesian PPI [46], FAB-PPI [47] and R-AutoEval+ [48] target small n, and R-AutoEval+ falls back to real data only when the proxy is poor. Kluger et al. [49] handle stratified and clustered designs with a bootstrap. Egami et al. [50] (DSL) state the design condition: labelling probabilities must be known. Lee et al. [51] correct binary judge rates with sensitivity and specificity (Rogan-Gladen) and show that PPI-style estimators become biased under label shift.

**How this repo relates.**
- *Is an application, not a method.* Our "classic PPI" is the difference estimator [34, 41] with a finite-population correction. Our PPI++ in `ppi_mean_finite` is the regression (GREG) estimator. Our "cross-fit" variant is a two-fold cross-PPI [36]. The rule "use classic PPI below about 25 labels" is a domain confirmation of Eyre and Madras [38].
- *Matches theory on width.* Our 9 to 23 % narrower intervals sit where Dorner et al. [39] and Chaganty et al. [40] predict. For wav2vec2 under phone+noise5, rho = 0.83 gives a ceiling width ratio of sqrt(1 - rho^2) = 0.56, which is exactly what we observe.
- *A distinctive proxy:* our "prediction" is the same metric measured on a simulated twin of the same script (TTS plus the same channel and ASR), not a model's guess of the label. Formally any auxiliary variable works, but I found no paper that uses paired simulation-to-real twins as the PPI proxy, and no application of PPI to ASR or voice-agent evaluation.
- *Calibration follow-up:* our follow-up already reproduces two known results. Inverse-MSE fidelity weights keep the bias and the narrow CI. A per-voice linear correction fitted and evaluated on the same labels under-covers (74 to 90 %), which is the regression-estimator optimism that cross-fitting [36] and shrinkage [38] address.
- *The voice-weighting problem is MultiPPI's problem* [44, 45]: seven correlated proxies of one gold target. Testing whether variance-optimal voice weights transfer from LibriSpeech to FLEURS would be a new empirical result.

## 5. Choosing which sentences to record

**What is known.** Active statistical inference [52] records each item with a known probability pi chosen from model uncertainty, and debiases with inverse-probability weighting. For a mean, the oracle pi is proportional to sqrt(E[(Y - f)^2 | x]), mixed with uniform. Li, Zrnic and Candes [53] show that active sampling can be noisier than uniform when the uncertainty scores are poor, and give a robust mixture that is never worse than uniform. Sfyraki and Wang [54] find that the best query probability tends towards uniform at the budget. Gligoric et al. [55] cut human annotations by over 25 % with confidence-driven selection, using hundreds of labels. Angelopoulos et al. [56] derive cost-optimal policies for a cheap and an expensive rater; fixed-rate policies are robust, and active gains need accurate uncertainty, which is hard in a cold start. Active Testing [57] is the ML-evaluation precursor.

**How this repo relates.** The follow-up in `results/followups/calibration/` found that active designs shrink the *reported* CI by 15 to 19 % but the *actual* error by only 1 to 7 %, with coverage down to 83 to 90 %. That is what [53, 54, 56] predict for a label-free uncertainty score in a cold start. Present it as a replication in a new domain, not a discovery. Our Poisson design is the Zrnic and Candes estimator. Our sampling rule differs from theirs: pi is linear in the score, mixed only 20 % with uniform. Our stratified design uses more and smaller strata than the stratified-PPI papers recommend (see `results/followups/LITERATURE-ACTIONS.md`).

## 6. TTS rendering errors and how to detect them

**What is known.** In software testing, Lau et al. [58] showed that 21 to 34 % of ASR failures found with TTS input are false alarms. These failures disappear when a human reads the same text. The rate depends on the ASR (wav2vec2 has the most) and on the TTS. Their false-alarm estimator reaches about 97 % F1. CrossASR and CrossASR++ [59] cross-reference several ASRs: when every ASR fails, the case is "indeterminable" because the TTS may have produced invalid audio. Luo and Wan [60] show the limit of round-trip checks: an ASR language model can repair a misreading. In their audit of 110 high-risk Chinese news cases, the round trip exposed only 9 of the 55 real reading errors. Yu and Kang [61] show that ASRs from the same lineage agree for non-independent reasons, so judges should come from different families. Earlier work on reference-free ASR error detection used disagreement between complementary recognizers [62], and ROVER [63] votes over aligned outputs. Learned TTS error locators exist [64]. SP-MCQA [65] shows that low WER does not guarantee key information is rendered correctly. For human checks, the SUS test [136] measures TTS intelligibility by having listeners transcribe semantically unpredictable sentences, so that context cannot repair a misheard word; listener transcription of this kind is the reference against which ASR round trips are judged. For combining noisy raters without gold labels, Dawid-Skene [66] assumes conditional independence, and Snorkel [67] models correlations between sources. Panels of diverse LLM judges beat a single judge in [68].

**How this repo relates.**
- *Not new as a method.* Our R3/R2 rule is a word-level, human-referenced version of Lau et al.'s false-alarm criterion [58]. Our caller-set rule (all models miss on one voice, the other voices are recognized) is CrossASR's "indeterminable" logic [59] extended with agreement across voices.
- *What we add:* validation of the flag against matched human audio; per-voice rates; and the link from rendering errors to distorted model comparisons and safety keywords. Removing flagged MMS words halves the MMS gap, and macOS Daniel's "help" read as "hell" drives the lowest-recall keyword for both strong models. The prior work treats false alarms as a testing-efficiency problem; it does not tie them to ranking distortion or keyword recall.
- *Our three ASRs are three distinct families* (encoder-decoder, TDT transducer, CTC), which is what [61] recommends. MMS-TTS is a VITS model; it shares only an organisation and training data lineage with wav2vec2, a weaker coupling than [61] studies. Our same-family check (FINDINGS section 7) addresses it.
- *Our counts are lower bounds* for two reasons: the rule is conservative, and language-model masking hides misreadings [60]. Only a listening audit with a full denominator can estimate the miss rate.
- *Our "diverse panel" result runs against* the jury intuition of [68]. For acoustic proxies, an unweighted panel of seven voices produced false confidence.

## 7. Phantom speech on non-speech audio, and guards

**What is known.** Whisper's own paper [69] gates no-speech segments with no_speech_prob > 0.6 and avg_logprob < -1, and calls its decoding heuristics a workaround. Koenecke et al. [70] found invented phrases in about 1 % of Whisper API transcriptions of real speech, 38 % of them harmful, more often for speakers with long non-vocal stretches. Barański et al. [71] ran Whisper large-v3 on 301,317 non-speech files: 40.3 % produced text. "Thank you" was 24.76 % of hallucinations and "thanks for watching" 10.32 %. Silero VAD was the best simple guard (WER 8.0 against 104.8 unprocessed on padded speech), and a bag of hallucinations (BoH) plus delooping helped further. Their list is released. Calm-Whisper [72] measured 99.97 % hallucination on UrbanSound8K for Whisper against 13.52 % for a CTC Conformer. It scores any non-empty transcript as a hallucination, and it cites Silero VAD v5 at only 61 % utterance-level accuracy on ESC-50.

Atwany et al. [73] define a Hallucination Error Rate (HER) and show that WER can hide hallucinations in either direction. Frieske and Shi [74] define ASR hallucination as fluent output unrelated to the audio. HALAS [75] labels hallucinations on real earnings calls for seven models, including Whisper large-v3-turbo (29.4 %) and Parakeet-TDT v2 (33.7 %); "thank you" is among the phrases shared by all seven. Jasiński et al. [76] find that a logistic regression on Whisper's own avg_logprob, compression ratio and no_speech_prob reaches only 23.6 % F1 on hallucinations *inside real speech*. Aparin et al. [77] argue, without testing it on these scalars, that phantom text is often confident and passes the default gate. Abbasihafshejani and Jadliwala [78] report each guard as a pair: hallucination rate on non-speech, and false rejection of real speech. SHALLOW [79] scores hallucinations on four axes. WhisperX [80] made VAD pre-segmentation standard practice. McKinnon et al. [81] find Silero better than WebRTC VAD. Sayed et al. [82] show that CTC and transducer models fail as garble rather than as fluent invented text. Whisper-AT [131] shows that Whisper's representation is not noise-invariant but correlated with non-speech sounds, so that Whisper recognizes speech conditioned on the noise type, which fits a phantom rate that depends on the noise. Distil-Whisper [132] reports fewer hallucinations than Whisper on long-form audio. Barański et al. [71] decode greedily at temperature 0 with other parameters at their defaults, and test openai-whisper's hallucination_silence_threshold (20 s) as a mitigation. The paper does not name its code path, but the defaults of openai-whisper's transcribe() include the no-speech gate, so their 40.3 % was probably measured with the gate on. Keyword spotting reports false alarms per hour of non-target audio [133], the natural unit for phantom keywords on a line that is mostly silence. The two VAD guards are released software: Silero VAD [134] (8 and 16 kHz) and the WebRTC VAD through py-webrtcvad [135].

**How this repo relates.**
- *Replicates.* Our 33 of 100 noise clips with text from Whisper (Wilson 95 % CI 24.6 to 42.7 %), 26 of them "thank you", plus runaway loops, replicate [71] and [72]. It is not a new phenomenon, and the numbers are not like for like: ours is the raw decoder with the gate off, theirs probably went through the default gate. Update (13/10/2026, `results/followups/phantom/v2`): the 33 is now moot. With `mlx_whisper.transcribe` 0.4.3 defaults the rate is 95 to 100 of 100; the gate never fires for large-v3-turbo there, so gated and ungated are the same; the 33 came from non-standard window padding in our raw decoder. 95 to 100 % is consistent with Calm-Whisper's 99.97 % [72].
- *Possibly new:* the rate after a simulated telephone line, and the three-family comparison on identical clips (main run: Whisper 33, wav2vec2 21, Parakeet 2; update 13/10/2026, v2 non-speech set excluding bot echo: Whisper 86.7 % raw decoder, wav2vec2 29.4 %, Parakeet 16.4 %, about 30 % on short clips and 2 % on long ones). I found no published non-speech rate for Parakeet-TDT v2. HALAS [75] shows that Parakeet hallucinates on real speech as often as Whisper, so our claim must be "robust to non-speech", not "hallucinates less", and after the v2 run only "robust to long non-speech clips".
- *Two checks I ran while writing this, both relevant to tonight's guard study:*
  - **The 33 % was measured with Whisper's built-in gate off.** `asr._whisper_batch` calls `decode()` directly, with no temperature fallback and no no-speech gating, and discards `no_speech_prob` and `avg_logprob`. An off-the-shelf user of the default long-form `transcribe()` path gets the gate of [69]. The paper must say so and report both numbers.
  - **The released BoH list (294 entries) does not contain a bare "thank you".** It has "thanks for watching", "thank you for watching" and "thank you very much". Matched exactly against our 33 Whisper outputs, it catches 1. Adopting BoH "as published" would not remove our dominant phantom phrase. Adding "thank you" by hand would delete legitimate caller speech: two of our benign scripts contain "thank you".
- *The emergency-line framing seems new.* Safety-critical hallucination work covers aphasia [70], earnings calls [75] and police radio [105] (accuracy only). I found no study of phantom text on an emergency line next to keyword recall.

## 8. Telephone channel simulation

**What is known.** Aurora-2 [83] set the protocol: 8 kHz, G.712 filtering, real noises on a fixed SNR grid, with held-out noises (set B) and a held-out filter (set C). The ITU-T Software Tools Library (G.191) [84] holds the reference implementations: the sv56 P.56 speech voltmeter, IRS and G.712 filters, G.711 Appendix I concealment and G.722 concealment. Paired real-telephone corpora exist: NTIMIT [85], STC-TIMIT [86] and WTIMIT [88]. Kamper and Niesler [87] used NTIMIT to show that a simulated channel still falls short of the real one, and that the noise model matters most. Vu et al. [89] found that real telephony data behaves like the most distorted simulated codecs. Fernández-Gallego and Toledano [90] found packet loss more damaging than low-bitrate codecs, and G.711 concealment helpful only when the ASR was trained on concealed speech. Dissen et al. [91] show that large Whisper tolerates zero-filled frame loss up to about 20 %. The Interspeech 2022 PLC Challenge [92] released real packet-loss traces from conferencing calls and scored systems partly with ASR. Bochner et al. [93] found packet loss the most harmful impairment for telephone captions. ASVspoof 2021 [94] transmitted its evaluation data over real codecs through a PBX and one real PSTN call. Behringer et al. [95] find classical codecs more noise-robust than neural ones. Basu et al. [96] is a current example of telephony robustness claims made from simulated codecs only. Switchboard [97] remains the standard real-telephony benchmark. VOiCES [123] re-recorded LibriSpeech speech in real furnished rooms with played-back noise and twelve microphones, a real-acoustics counterpart of LibriSpeech. The bursty-loss model behind Gilbert-Elliott frame drops goes back to Gilbert and Elliott [139]. Cooke's glimpsing model [138] predicts intelligibility in noise from the proportion of spectro-temporal regions where speech dominates the noise; it is the standard tool for asking why the same SNR does different damage to different talkers.

**How this repo relates.**
- *Follows standard practice.* Our phone line, noise at P.56 active-level SNR measured after the line, and Appendix I-style concealment are Aurora- and STL-style, but implemented in Python, not with the reference tools. Our SNR definition is stricter than Speech Robust Bench's whole-signal norm.
- *Is close to tau-Voice* [14]: our "realistic" channel is nearly its preset.
- *Lacks what this literature stresses.* Cellular codecs (AMR, GSM-FR) are missing, and the channel is never checked against a real transmission. By [87, 89], our lab-vs-real gaps are probably lower bounds on two counts: synthetic voice and simulated channel.
- *Has a mechanism check available.* Our hypothesis for Kokoro's smaller noise damage is its steadier level and lack of room tone, which would leave more glimpses at the same active-level SNR. Glimpse proportion [138] per voice and sentence under the same noise tests it without new ASR runs.
- *Is thinly covered elsewhere:* packet loss with codec-native concealment on current foundation ASR. Our finding that 5 % loss costs nothing measurable with either concealment fits Dissen et al. [91] for large models.

## 9. Recall of critical terms in emergency and medical speech

**What is known.** Keyword error rate rises faster than WER as transcripts degrade [98], though it did not change two analytics tasks. Moser et al. [99] benchmark ASR on German emergency dialogues synthesized with Piper TTS under noise, with a medical-term WER. They have no human baseline, and they state that TTS may misjudge difficulty in either direction. MedWER [100] gives a reproducible, phrase-aware critical-term WER with a pinned term list. Ellis et al. [101] show that WER correlates poorly with clinicians' risk labels. On real calls, the Copenhagen studies [102] found a speech-based model more sensitive than dispatchers (84.1 % vs 72.5 %) but with lower PPV. In the randomized trial, its alerts did not significantly improve dispatcher recognition. Ryu et al. [103] report more false alarms for call-for-help detection in noisy deployment. RescueSpeech [104] and police radio [105] are among the few public human-speech corpora in this domain.

**How this repo relates.**
- *Audits the practice of Moser et al.* [99], the closest published analogue of our caller-set experiment (Piper TTS, noise at SNR, Whisper turbo, emergency terms, conclusions from TTS alone). Our voice spread, rendering errors and the understated noise cost show why TTS-only keyword benchmarks can mislead.
- *Has the same gap they do:* no human audio for the caller scripts and no real emergency calls.
- *Already matches keywords phrase-aware,* as MedWER does (`stats._contains`).

## What is new here, honestly

Established by others, and to be cited rather than claimed:
- TTS test sets can reorder ASR models [1].
- TTS audio is usually easier and gives optimistic WER [2, 3, 4, 5]. Our Kokoro result replicates this; our other five voices qualify it.
- TTS-found ASR failures are often voice artefacts [58]. Cross-ASR agreement singles out TTS faults [59].
- Whisper produces text on non-speech, mostly "thank you", and Silero VAD is the strongest simple guard [71, 72].
- PPI, PPI++, cross-fitting, stratification and active inference, together with their small-n behaviour and the size of their gain [34 to 57].
- Telephone codec, packet-loss and noise simulation as such [6, 14, 83, 84].

Also established, and missing from the first draft of this section:
- Several TTS voices against a matched human reading of the same LibriSpeech sentences, with several ASRs [58], and many TTS systems against a ground-truth row with a fixed ASR judge [127, 128].
- Treating a sample of stimuli as fixed makes tests anti-conservative [106 to 112]; resample or model the stimuli as random [28, 107, 108, 113].
- Simulated test conditions can mislead about real ones: noise and microphones [25], reverberation [121], noise suppression [122], robot policies [120].
- Voice panels without a human anchor are read as robustness [3, 129].

Apparently new, after targeted searches, each with its closest prior work:
1. **The voice as a factor in comparisons between ASR models, against a matched human reading, with inference.** For each voice we test whether the gap between two ASR models on TTS matches the gap on human speech of the same sentences (speaker-cluster bootstrap, DiD, Holm). For the two close strong models, only MMS (all four conditions) and Piper ryan (phone) distort the comparison nominally, and nothing survives Holm. For pairs with a large gap, the voice rescales it from 0.37x to 3.72x, Holm-significant in 60 of 98 decisions. *Closest prior:* Lau et al. [58] (four TTS x five ASRs x human on LibriSpeech, per-cell WER, no inference on model gaps); 2M-BELEBELE [1] (one TTS, rank swaps without CIs); the TTS evaluation convention [127, 128] (many TTS, one ASR). *Limits:* the 0.37x to 3.72x range is a descriptive extreme over many cells, and every large-gap pair involves the in-domain wav2vec2 control, whose human advantage is partly a training-domain advantage.
2. **Averaging a voice panel without resampling voices gives significance the human data cannot confirm.** The 7-voice mean declares Parakeet better on three phone or noisy conditions where the human CI includes 0, while its DiD is never significant. The mechanism is not new: it is the language-as-fixed-effect fallacy [106] applied to TTS voices, and BOOT-BOTH [28] is the standard correction (update 13/10/2026: it widens the interval about 2x but leaves the winner on 2 or 3 of 3 conditions; see `CLAIMS-STATUS.md`). What is new is the empirical demonstration for TTS voice panels in ASR and voice-agent evaluation, where agreement between voices is read as reliability [3, 129]. *Closest prior:* Clark [106]; Deutsch et al. [28]; multi-prompt evaluation [113]; VoiceBench's Kendall's W [3]. *Limits:* the human gaps point the same way (+0.60 [-0.04, +1.29] on realistic), so the lab may be right and the human arm underpowered. The defensible claim is "overconfident", not "wrong", until an equivalence test [118] and a voice-resampled CI are reported.
3. **The source x channel interaction, against a matched human reading.** A channel's cost estimated on TTS differs from its cost on human speech for the same sentences: clean Kokoro shows about half the noise damage for the strong models, and macOS voices overstate the phone line and muffling for the control. *Closest prior:* Yang et al. [12] and the older synthetic-speech literature [137] (human listeners, TTS in noise, opposite directions across TTS generations); VoiceBench [3], VoxEval [129], WildSpeech-Bench [130] and Moser et al. [99] (TTS plus noise or channel, no human anchor); CHiME-3 [25], REVERB [121] and DNS [122] (simulated vs real channel, not source). *Limits:* the mechanism is a hypothesis until a glimpse-proportion check [138].
4. **Phantom-speech rates after a telephone line, compared across three ASR families on identical clips, framed as false alarms on an emergency line.** A replication with a new channel and a new comparison, not a new phenomenon. *Closest prior:* Barański et al. [71], Calm-Whisper [72], HALAS [75], Whisper-AT [131]. *Limits:* Whisper was decoded without its gate. Update (13/10/2026): measured; the gate never fires for large-v3-turbo under mlx-whisper 0.4.3, and the standard-settings rate is 95 to 100 % (`results/followups/phantom/v2`).
5. **PPI with simulation-to-real twins as the proxy, for speech.** The application and the per-voice, per-channel rho values seem new; the estimator is not. *Closest prior:* AutoEval [37] and control variates for NLG metrics [40]; SIMPLER [120] pairs simulation and reality for robot policies but does not use PPI. *Limits:* no targeted search on PPI or control variates in speech or TTS evaluation was possible in the second pass.
6. **Rendering errors linked to ranking distortion and keyword recall,** validated against human audio. The detection method is not new. *Closest prior:* Lau et al. [58], CrossASR [59], Luo and Wan [60].

What a speech reviewer will attack first, in order: the 33 % measured with the gate off (answered 13/10/2026, in the direction that makes the problem larger: 95 to 100 % with standard settings); "no winner" read from CIs that include 0; the 0.37x to 3.72x range resting on the in-domain control; seven voices that are four engines; "no single voice flips" without a minimum detectable effect; LibriSpeech readers as "humans" for an emergency line; channels never checked against a real transmission.

Not covered, and reviewers will raise it: caller behaviour; streaming and full-duplex pipelines [14, 17]; real telephone transmission [87, 94]; real emergency audio [102]; a human baseline outside the models' training domain (FLEURS has since been run, `results/followups/fleurs`; the 2M-BELEBELE precedent [1] uses the same corpus).
